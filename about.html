Aboutthe codex

What this is

Atlas Codex is a reference library. Each entry covers what a compound is, its structure, how it acts on the body, its risks, and the human clinical record behind it. Every claim carries a grade for the evidence behind it.

How an entry is made

Every compound entry has the same sections in the same order, so two entries can be read side by side: risk, interactions, monitoring, the human clinical record, dosing, pharmacokinetics, mechanism and references. Where a section has nothing in it, the entry says so instead of leaving it out.

Entries are written from primary sources: regulator labels, published trial reports, systematic reviews and the papers they cite. Forums, vendor pages and coaching protocols are left out on purpose. Where a creator's view and the published record disagree, the codex follows the record.

Dosing holds only two kinds of number: the dose on an approved regulator label, and the dose a published trial actually gave. If neither exists, the panel says there is no established human dose and leaves the gap open.

Monitoring lists which markers a compound makes worth watching, and why. It leaves out target ranges, because reading a result is a clinical decision.

Evidence grades

How strong the evidence behind a claim is. The grade sits beside the claim, not in a footnote.

  • Human RCTRandomized controlled trials in people.
  • EstablishedSettled facts: a regulator's label, well-replicated pharmacology, or a matter of record, such as a trial never having been published.
  • Human observationalCohort studies, case series and case reports in people, without randomization.
  • AnimalIn vitroPreclinical only: animal studies, or cells and tissue in a dish.
  • AnecdotalUser reports, with no controlled data.
  • ContestedPublished evidence points both ways.
  • UnknownNo usable data.

Risk levels

How serious a risk is. Read it together with the grade beside it.

  • High riskSerious harm, or harm common enough that it should shape the decision, is on record.
  • Moderate riskA real harm that is usually uncommon, dose-related or manageable with monitoring.
  • Low riskMinor, rare or reversible on the record so far.
  • Unknown riskThe record is too thin to rate it.

The level says how serious a risk is. The badge beside it says how strong the evidence for it is. A high risk graded Animal is a warning from animal work, not a measured rate in people. An unknown risk is a gap, not a reassurance.

Profile scores

Profile bars are my editorial judgements on a 1 to 5 scale, relative to similar compounds. Red rows are attributes where a higher score is worse. Risk load is an overall reading of how much harm is on record at the doses studied.

Where a compound has no randomized human trial and little other human data, a low risk score is drawn without colour and marked "Thin human record". Little is known, which is not the same as little risk.

Visitors can add their own scores. These are stored only in their own browser and never change the codex's scores.

Status labels

  • ApprovedApproved as a medicine, or as a cosmetic ingredient, in at least one jurisdiction. Where that is not the US, or only as a cosmetic, the label says where.
  • InvestigationalIn formal clinical development.
  • Research onlyNot approved for human use. Evidence is mostly preclinical.
  • DiscontinuedWas once an approved medicine but has been withdrawn or is no longer marketed.
  • VeterinaryApproved for animals only.
  • Never approvedNever approved as a medicine anywhere, for example designer steroids.
  • BannedProhibited for human use or unlawful in supplements, for example DNP and DMAA.
  • SupplementSold as a dietary supplement rather than an approved drug.
  • CompoundedMade to prescription by a compounding pharmacy rather than sold as an approved product.
  • CosmeticSold as a cosmetic or personal care product, not as a drug.

What gets an entry

A compound gets an entry when people who experiment on themselves are using it or asking about it, or when an approved medicine is commonly used alongside those compounds. Guides cover how to read the evidence. Being in the codex means a compound is discussed. It does not mean it works, and it is not a recommendation.

Who writes it

The codex is written and edited by one person, the site's owner. No entry has been reviewed by a clinician or an outside expert. That is why every claim carries its grade and its reference: so you can check the source instead of taking my word for it.

Money and independence

The codex earns a commission when readers use code ATLAS at Half Nattys, and the disclosure sits beside every affiliate link. You can support it on the Support me page.

Whether the source sells a compound has no bearing on its entry. Risks, grades and dosing are written from the record, and the record outranks the codex's own revenue. A listing is never a recommendation, and banned compounds never get a shop prompt.

Corrections and requests

Corrections and entry requests go to the codex's public issue tracker on GitHub. Every entry has a "Report an error in this entry" link that opens a report for that entry. A correction is most useful with the source that shows it: a label, a trial report or a PubMed link. Opening a report needs a free GitHub account.

Glossary

Terms the entries use, in plain words.

Randomized controlled trial (RCT)
A trial that assigns people to a treatment or a comparison by chance, so the groups differ only in what they were given.
Placebo
An inactive comparison treatment made to look like the real one.
Double-blind, open-label
In a double-blind trial neither the participants nor the researchers know who got what until the end. In an open-label trial everyone knows, which makes subjective results less reliable.
Endpoint
The outcome a trial was designed to measure. A surrogate endpoint, such as a lab value, can move without the outcome that matters changing.
Phase 1, 2 and 3
Phase 1 tests safety and dosing in small groups. Phase 2 looks for an effect and the right dose. Phase 3 tests the effect in large groups and is usually what approval rests on.
Observational study
A study that follows people who chose or were given a treatment, without assigning it. It can show an association, not a cause.
Systematic review, meta-analysis
A review that gathers every trial on one question, and often pools their results. Cochrane reviews are one widely used series.
Half-life
The time it takes for the amount in the blood to fall by half.
Bioavailability
The share of a dose that reaches the bloodstream.
Cmax, Tmax
The peak blood level after a dose, and how long it takes to get there.
SC, IM, IV
Subcutaneous (under the skin), intramuscular (into a muscle) and intravenous (into a vein).
CYP enzymes
Liver enzymes, such as CYP3A4 and CYP2D6, that break down many drugs. A compound that blocks or speeds one up can change the levels of others.
Label
A medicine's regulator-approved prescribing information: its uses, doses, warnings and interactions.
503A, 503B
The two sections of US law that cover compounding pharmacies. 503A pharmacies compound for individual prescriptions. 503B outsourcing facilities make larger batches under closer FDA oversight.
WADA
The World Anti-Doping Agency, which publishes the list of substances prohibited in sport.
COA
Certificate of analysis: a lab report on one batch of a product. It is only as good as the lab, the tests run and the batch it covers. Reading a COA.
HPLC
High-performance liquid chromatography, the usual purity test. It measures how much of a sample is the main compound. On its own it is weak evidence of which compound that is.
Mass spectrometry
A test that identifies a molecule by its mass. Paired with HPLC it can confirm identity as well as purity.
Endotoxin
Fragments of bacterial cell walls. Injected, they can cause fever and shock. A purity test does not detect them.
SERM, aromatase inhibitor
Drugs that block estrogen's effect at its receptor (a SERM), or block its production from testosterone (an aromatase inhibitor).
PCT
Post-cycle therapy: drugs used after anabolic steroids to restart the body's own testosterone production.
HPG axis
The hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis, the hormone chain that controls testosterone production and that outside androgens suppress.
Tanimoto similarity
A score from 0 to 1 for how many structural features two molecules share. The compare page uses it.
